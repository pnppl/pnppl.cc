## Intro

I love this series. The last time I saw it was with a woman I was dating, which is probably the best way to watch it.

The experimental, surreal, campy, and just plain silly elements can be a little hard to parse if you get too caught up in figuring them out. Just relax and give the show time to grow on you — you'll come to understand it on a vibrational level, if not a literal one. Then you can ponder the details afterwards and on rewatches.

As for this document, it's a messy, lightly edited mishmash of notes and analysis and whatnot.


## New translation

I watched a [[recent fansub|https://haru-dipthong.tumblr.com/post/791319160102830080/haru-dipthongs-utena-fansub-masterpost]] which is written from a nonbinary perspective. I vaguely recall the translation(s) I watched before having some awkward/confusing moments (like most anime). This one felt completely natural.

Things that stood out:

- "-sama" is translated as "Master", which is a bold choice but it works. I can understand why they're controversial, but I lean toward approving of these sorts of localizations. Just because I recognize the honorifics doesn't mean I understand all the connotations; also, I can still *hear* the honorific just fine, so the localization provides an additional perspective and doesn't take anything away. (I do find it weirdly irritating how they put the names into Western order though. I've always found it jarring to have two words just switch places. Subs basically always do that, unfortunately.)
- Utena says "dude" sometimes. It feels perfectly natural and in character.
- The fuck word is used.
- I feel like I can lean in harder to interpreting the show feeling confident that I won't get hung up by a bad translation; when it sounds weird in English I know it's *supposed to*.

A cool thing about this fansub is there's a page for each episode with notes on translation decisions and the analysis of the series' themes that informed them. Apparently Utena uses masculine terms to refer to herself/themself which usually gets lost in translation.

> > Look dude. Despite how I seem, I’m just a normal girl.
>
> I really wanted to preserve the hilarious contradiction present in the Japanese where Utena is saying they’re a normal girl while still using boku. They’ve got one foot on the accelerator and one foot on the brake of their gender with this line — ending it in a feminine なの particle.
>
> — [[Haru Dipthong: translation notes, ep. 3|https://haru-dipthong.tumblr.com/post/763038485414395905/my-utena-fansub-ep3]]

## What does it meeeeeean?

### What does Miki's stopwatch represent?

It's a rhythmic element used to punctuate scenes, but is there any deeper meaning here?

It seems that Miki is taking a measure of the other characters, holding themself apart as a judge and observer rather than participant. This would fit neatly with the trans reading, with Miki constantly measuring her social role to make sure she's performing gender correctly.

It also might just refer to how Miki is the only Student Council member who is relatively nice and normal and not a conniving creep, and hence acts as a "straight man" barometer for the others.

Nanami actually asks Miki about it and Miki is evasive. Perhaps it's nothing more than a running joke.


### What do the spinning roses represent?

Roses are synonymous with romantic love (at least in the west). They're also a staple of visual euphemism for vulva. And the sapphic genre _Utena_ exists in is called "yuri", which literally means lily; lilies look very different from roses, but, you know, *flower*. So at first blush, "roses = gay" isn't much of a stretch, but the way they're used is subtler than that.

Sometimes the roses obviously represent love or attraction. Sometimes, it's more like they mean magic, or perhaps drama. Sometimes they indicate a scene is a memory. As the show goes on, you get a feel for them, but it's still hard to find a definition.

There's a very interesting part in one of the early duels — maybe the first one? — where Utena channels The Prince's spirit. When his form crosses into theirs, they're obscured by a spinning rose, then Utena magically wins the duel. The way the rose is used here feels like censorship of a sexual encounter! (It might just be a really low-budget way to indicate some magic shit is happening.)

While there is magic in the show, I don't tend to interpret it literally. The Power to Revolutionize the World seems like a metaphor for queer love and/or identity. So maybe it's correct to say that the roses represent magic, but the magic isn't literal.

There's also some fancy meta-textual type shit happening. Like, you get scenes with the roses where it seems like they indicate the *story itself* is magically acting on its participants, intervening mischievously to cause drama.

In episode 31, they seem to mean "now loading". They appear during the silent pauses between Touga/Akio making a pass at Nanami and her response.

[[Someone has catalogued them all.|https://satirist.org/essays/utena/spinning-rose-catalog.html]] Their interpretation is that they "introduce a character, or a new aspect of them".

## The story as I understand it

Long ago, Akio was a great warrior who slayed dragons. The people he protected demanded too much of him and it took a toll. To protect him from the villagers, Anthy cast a spell which unstuck them from time. It made them immortal and doomed her to endlessly relive being stabbed to death protecting her brother.

Anthy could leave this at any time, but she chooses to continue to endure it for her brother, who expects it from her as part of a regimen of abuse. The endless torment of the villager's swords also represents the ongoing pain caused by his abuse, which it is both part of and facilitates. Akio wants her to stay in her place so that he can access superpowers hidden behind a locked door.

In order to get into the door, he needs a powerful sword. He has already tried many of these, which break when he tries to cut the door open. These swords are summoned from the chests of duellists — would-be rescuers/owners of Anthy. The more you care about someone, the stronger the sword that person can summon from your chest, and the effect is heightened when the feeling is mutual. To this end, various fucked up rituals like the duels have been arranged by Akio to try to generate a powerful sword. Anthy has tried to numb herself against feeling anything for these people because she knows they will be used and then killed — by her, if necessary — and the swords that stab her also represent her pain associated with this: both the people she deprived herself of feeling for and the ones she cared about enough to scar her.

It's not clear what Akio wants with the power to revolutionize the world, or whether he even really wants it. He's satisfied with everything staying the way it is forever. If he ever cared about his sister, that is long past. He seems aware of what a bad person he has become and regrets it, but still expects her to be his sacrificial lamb forever, just so so he can enjoy himself at the top of the food pyramid. To maintain his position, he abuses her in many ways: by putting her through scenarios of play-acting at being someone *else's* slave, by sleeping with the people she cares about, and by sexually abusing her. (I suppose this could be consensual, but Akio systemically manipulates and exploits everyone around him and treats her like shit; while he has apparently gotten worse over time, one wonders if he was ever really a good person and when their relationship became incestuous.)

In any case, he can't get to it — but Utena can, because they are a sincerely good person who actually loves Anthy. Utena's nobility of character — their willingness to fight for the freedom of the one they love as their authentic self, in spite of constant setbacks and self-doubt — lets them open Anthy's heart to her need to love herself, leave Akio, and let herself experience true love again.

> So what I think is interesting is that while Utena is a woman, she is venturing into an essentially male "romance". I tried to translate that phrase "venturing into a romance" to "venturing into the world". I stipulated that until now, there had never been a girl venturing into the world, and through the stages of the story she becomes the first woman to discover "the end of the world". I tried to think of a story where, because she is the first to venture into the world, she is also the first to know that despair.
